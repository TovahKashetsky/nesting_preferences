Nest-Site Selection in Non-Eusocial Cavity-Nesting Bees

OUTDATED- I’VE BEEN USING “TK - Update”

Background

Strategies for raising offspring exist on a spectrum from cooperative breeding in tightly knit social groups to raising young in isolation who may not even encounter their parents or siblings after emerging. Raising young in proximity with others has benefits such as increased protection from predators and enhanced knowledge of local resources. However, proximity can increase the circulation of pathogens and foster competition if resources become scarce. Bees have adopted the entirety of this spectrum, as species range from solitary (e.g., most leafcutter bees: Megachile spp.) to advanced eusociality (e.g., honeybees: Apis spp.). Some species along this spectrum are facultatively social, meaning that within a population, they exhibit social polymorphism for their nesting strategy and thus can be solitary or social (e.g., some carpenter bees: Xylocopa spp.)

Bees will nest in the ground or in above-ground cavities, depending on the species. Nesting in proximity to others is common among ground-nesting bees. There are a few outlined explanations for why ground-nesting bee aggregations may occur. Bees of the same species are likely to prefer the same site characteristics; thus, one location may be appealing to several individuals. Additionally, resource limitation can encourage aggregations, as bees nest near floral resources, and nesting locations themselves are resources. Philopatry, the behaviour of returning to have offspring at the location of birth, could also be a driving factor in aggregations. Finally, individuals could be attracted to conspecifics, as their presence could signal good-quality habitat. Nesting aggregation preferences have yet to be explicitely tested in cavity0nesting bees, despite trapnests and bee hotels commonly being used for research and citizen-based conservation. While there is evidence that cavity-nesting Osmia bees in one Colorado populationdo not consider presence of other individuals while choosing where to nest (Groulx and Forrest 2018), it remains unclear how aggregation density and parasitism interact in nest-site selection of cavity-nesting bees.

Objectives

I began to address this research gap by studying nest-site selection of non-eusocial cavity-nesting bees by measuring their preferences for aggregations and avoidance of parasites. In the Greenbelt of Ottawa, I tested nesting preferences of orchard bees (Osmia spp.) and leafcutter bees (Megachile spp.). My two primary questions are:

  1. Do cavity-nesting bees have aggregation density preferences? If bees have aggregation density preferences, they could prefer nesting in higher density aggregations because the presence of nesting conspecifics could indicate the presence of high-quality nesting habitat. Alternatively, bees could avoid the presence of conspecifics if density increases parasitism risk, thus prefer nesting in lower aggregation densities. If cavity-nesting bees do not consider aggregation density while selecting a nesting location, bees must use other nest-site characteristics when choosing where to build a nest.

  2. How does parasitism interact with nesting preferences?

    A. I will investigate the relation between parasites and aggregation densities. Parasites might target higher densities of nests because the presence of many host nests indicates more opportunities for parasitism. On the other hand, parasites may target lower densities of nests because there could be fewer chances of directly encountering a host, which could interrupt the act of parasitizing a nest.

    B. I will measure how parasitism rates affect nesting preferences of bees. If parasites are density-dependent, bees may alter their own preferences in attempt to reduce chances of parasitism However, regardless if parasites are desinty-dependent, their presence alone may influence nest-site selection by bees directly avoiding parasites

Methods

I deployed trapnests containing 18 cavities each. The first half of the summer was dedicated towards the early-season orchard bees (Osmia spp.), while the second half was dedicated towards the later-season leafcutter bees (Megachile spp.). For each half of the summer, I deployed 36 trapnests spread evenly between two sites, for a total of 72. Trapnests were divided into three treatments to simulate variations in occupancy:

  • Low-occupancy treatment: the baseline of completed nests is zero. Once a bee nest is completed, we removed it for storage at the University of Ottawa in falcon tubes. The trapnest treatment should have little to no bee activity.

  • Mid-occupancy treatment: the baseline of completed nests was 6 out of 18 cavities occupied. Occupancy was simulated with pseudo nests built from old nesting material from real orchard or leafcutter bee nests, or the equivalent to what bees would use for building (mud for orchard bee season, leaves for leafcutter bee season). Once real nests were built, pseudo nests were removed to maintain baseline occupancy. If real nests surpassed the baseline occupancy, we removed them for storage in falcon tubes to maintain the baseline density.

  • High-occupancy treatment: the baseline of completed nests was 11 out of 18 cavities. Maintaining the baseline occupancy was identical to methods for the mid-occupancy treatment.

In total, I have ~164 Osmia nests and 58 Megachile nests (IDed based on nesting material).
